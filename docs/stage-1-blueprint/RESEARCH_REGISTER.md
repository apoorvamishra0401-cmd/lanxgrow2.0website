Method: web search plus direct retrieval of primary/original documents. Every source below was retrieved and read at the URL shown, unless explicitly marked **UNVERIFIED**. Secondary citations (a figure quoted by one source from another report) are labelled as such and are never recommended for publication.

**Publication codes**
- **P-Home / P-About** — suitable for the public site, with the stated caveat.
- **Background** — useful for internal reasoning and investor/Q&A context, not for public headline use.
- **Do-not-publish** — insufficient, outdated, commercial, or secondary; must not appear as a site claim.

## R1 — English reading among Indian youth (ASER 2023)

- **Exact claim:** Among youth aged 14–18 in rural India, **57.3% can read sentences in English**; of those, **73.5% can tell their meaning**. Separately, about **25% cannot read a Std II-level text fluently in their regional language**, and only **43.3% can do a 3-digit ÷ 1-digit division problem**.
- **Source:** *Annual Status of Education Report (ASER) 2023 'Beyond Basics'*. Publisher: ASER Centre / Pratham Foundation. Released 16 January 2024.
- **URL:** https://asercentre.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/ASER-2023-Main-Findings_Jan-16-2024-1.pdf
- **Population / geography / sample / method:** **Rural India only**. 34,745 youth aged 14–18, in 28 districts across 26 states (one rural district per major state; two in UP and MP). Nationwide, citizen-led household survey; one-on-one oral assessment with graded tools.
- **What it actually measures:** The ability to *read and understand simple written English sentences* — a literacy/decoding measure. It is a snapshot, not a trend test of speaking.
- **What it does NOT prove:** It does **not** measure spoken English, conversational fluency, listening, pronunciation or workplace communication. It does not cover urban India or private-school-only populations. It says nothing about causation or about any programme's effect. Because only 1–2 districts per state were sampled, district-level generalisation is limited.
- **Notes:** ASER is an NGO-run citizen survey, not a government census; its methodology is published and it is widely cited (including by national media). ASER itself frames the finding positively — "close to 60% youth can read simple English … an advantage that needs to be built on" — rather than as a failing.
- **Publication:** **P-About / Background.** If used, it must be quoted as *rural 14–18-year-olds' English reading*, never as "Indian students can't speak English".

## R2 — What employers rank as core skills (WEF Future of Jobs 2025)

- **Exact claim (2025):** The top core skills employers report are **analytical thinking** (7 in 10 companies; ≈69%), then **resilience, flexibility and agility** (≈67%), **leadership and social influence** (≈61%), **creative thinking** (≈57%), **motivation and self-awareness** (≈52%), **technological literacy** (≈51%), **empathy and active listening** (≈50%) and **curiosity and lifelong learning** (≈50%).
- **Source:** *The Future of Jobs Report 2025*, World Economic Forum, 7 January 2025 (Chapter 3: Skills outlook; Appendix: methodology).
- **URLs:** https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/in-full/3-skills-outlook/ · https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/in-full/appendix-6d9e5fce68/
- **Population / geography / sample / method:** Global employer survey — **1,043 unique company responses**, representing **>14.1 million employees**, **22 industry clusters** and **55 economies** (=88% of global GDP). Fielded May–September 2024. Target companies had **≥500 employees**. Excludes small enterprises and the informal sector.
- **What it actually measures:** Employer *opinion* about skills required by workers today and expected by 2030.
- **What it does NOT prove:** It is **global**, large-employer-only, and **does not rank English (or any single language) as a top skill** — "multi-lingualism" ranks low. It must **not** be used to claim "English is the most in-demand skill". It supports only the narrower, defensible point that **communication-adjacent interpersonal skills** (leadership and social influence, empathy and active listening) are highly valued, alongside cognitive and self-efficacy skills.
- **Publication:** **P-Home / P-About** for the *interpersonal-and-cognitive skills* framing only; **do-not-publish** any "English is #1" reading.

## R3 — Returns to English, and why they are hard to isolate (British Council, Erling 2014)

- **Exact claims:** English-language skills are "highly rewarded in the labour market", but returns "are heterogeneous and … accrue along with other socioeconomic variables such as gender, ethnicity, class and location"; "it is difficult to separate returns to English language skills from returns to quality education"; and much of the research concerns formal, high-level jobs, whereas the informal sector is up to ~90% of jobs in South Asia. The report adds that the evidence "**do[es] not suggest that use of local languages hinder economic development**" and that local languages may be of particular value in informal labour markets.
- **Source:** Elizabeth J. Erling, *The Role of English in Skills Development in South Asia: Policies, Interventions and Existing Evidence*, British Council, June 2014.
- **URL:** https://www.britishcouncil.in/sites/default/files/role_of_english_in_skills_development_in_south_asia_inside.pdf
- **Population / geography / sample / method:** A **literature/evidence review** of South Asia, synthesising primary econometric studies.
- **What it actually measures:** A synthesis of correlation evidence on education/English and economic outcomes.
- **What it does NOT prove:** No causal claim that teaching English *causes* higher earnings or employment. The document is now **~12 years old** and must not be presented as a current estimate.
- **Publication:** **Background.** Excellent for internal rigour and for the About page's careful "knowledge vs use" framing; not for a public statistic.

### R3a — Wage figures cited *within* R3 (secondary)
- **Claim:** Azam, Chin & Prakash (2010), using the India Human Development Survey 2005, found fluent English raised male hourly wages by ~34% vs no English; "a little English" by ~13%. Euromonitor (2010) reported a 5–15% (Bangladesh) / 10–15% (Pakistan) salary gap.
- **Status:** **Secondary citation only** — original papers were not retrieved in this pass; measures were partly self-reported and dated. **Do-not-publish.**

## R4 — Entry-level language requirements: a known evidence gap (British Council, Prince & Singh 2015)

- **Exact claims:** The report states there has been "**little direct research which clearly identifies the language requirements of entry level jobs**", and that while there is "a general anecdotal feeling that there is a positive correlation between English language and mobility, there is actually **little hard evidence to show a causal link** between English and economic development".
- **Source:** Emma-Sue Prince & S. Manish Singh, *English Skills for Employability: Setting Common Standards* (a research report), British Council India with Manipal City & Guilds, Trinity College London and the National Skills Development Agency. Research conducted 2014–15; report launched New Delhi, 9 June 2015.
- **URLs:** https://www.britishcouncil.in/sites/default/files/esfe_report_final.pdf · launch note: https://www.britishcouncil.in/about/press/english-skills-employability-common-standards
- **Population / geography / sample / method:** India; **action research** with three Sector Skills Councils (healthcare, hospitality, construction) and a review of the National Skills Qualifications Framework.
- **What it actually measures:** Where and how English fits vocational qualification standards; the state of evidence.
- **What it does NOT prove:** It is a small, sector-specific standards study, and it is ~11 years old.
- **Publication:** **Background.** Most useful as an honest statement of the *evidence gap* — and as a warning against overclaiming.

## R5 — Seven to ten years of English tuition, modest communicative outcome (British Council, Borg/Padwad/Kalita Nath 2022)

- **Exact claims:** In India "children learn it for seven to ten years at school", yet "the over-riding sense that persists is that **the quality of English teaching and learning across primary and early secondary education is modest**, a factor that feeds into low English proficiency at subsequent stages of education, including among university students and in the population more generally". Teaching "practices … remain didactic", with speaking and listening "**not given substantial space** during English lessons", and English-medium instruction used "often as a status symbol".
- **Source:** Simon Borg, Amol Padwad & Pranjana Kalita Nath, *English language teaching, learning and assessment in India: Policies and practices in the school education system*, British Council, April 2022, ISBN 978-1-915280-05-3.
- **URL:** https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/sites/teacheng/files/ELT%20learning%20and%20assessment%20in%20India_April%202022_new2.pdf
- **Population / geography / sample / method:** India (school education); **desk-based review** of policy documents, reports and academic papers, supplemented by "a small number of interviews" with teachers and teacher educators — explicitly **not generalisable**.
- **What it actually measures:** A qualitative synthesis of policy and classroom practice.
- **What it does NOT prove:** It is not a measurement of learner ability and cannot be quoted as a percentage or as representative evidence. It is a description of *practice and perception*.
- **Publication:** **P-About / Background** — this is the single most useful source for the "knowing English vs. using English" gap, provided it is described as a review of practice, not as a statistic.

## R6 — National Achievement Survey 2021 (NCERT / Ministry of Education)

- **Exact claims:** Administered 12 November 2021 to classes 3, 5, 8 and 10 across **3.4 million students**, **1,18,274 schools**, **720 districts**, all **36 states/UTs**, via competency-based booklets scored with Item Response Theory. For **Class 10**, the national average **English** score corresponds to roughly **43%** (reported), and NCERT's Class X cycle-2 report gives a national average of **253/500** for English, with ~**44%** of learners in the 201–250 band and average sub-scores of **38% remembering, 33% understanding, 34% applying**.
- **Source:** National Achievement Survey 2021, NCERT under the Ministry of Education, released 25 May 2022.
- **URLs:** https://nas.gov.in/report-card/2021 · https://ncert.nic.in/pdf/NAS/NationalReportClassXCycle2.pdf
- **Population / geography / sample / method:** Government, government-aided, private-recognised and central-government schools nationwide; large representative sample.
- **What it actually measures:** Achievement against **curriculum learning outcomes** in English as a school subject, across two content domains — **reading comprehension and grammar**.
- **What it does NOT prove:** It is **not** a test of spoken communication and says nothing about conversational ability. The 2021 round was affected by COVID-19 school closures. Class 10 was not assessed in the 2017 round, so no 2021-vs-2017 comparison exists for Class 10. The 2021 scores are a **2021** measurement, not a current estimate.
- **Publication:** **P-About / Background**, framed strictly as *written English achievement in school*, never as speaking ability.

## R7 — Employer perception of communication skills, Bangalore (ISEC 2024)

- **Exact claims:** Among **50 employers** in Bangalore across IT, manufacturing, factory and entrepreneurship, **100% required communication skills**, while employers judged that only **20% (IT), 40% (manufacturing), 0% (factory) and 16% (entrepreneurs)** of MBA graduates possessed them — described by the authors as the largest requirement/possession gap among the skills measured.
- **Source:** Dinu Raj R & Marchang Reimeingam, *Employers' Perception of Skills and Learning Mismatch of MBA Graduates in Bangalore*, **ISEC Working Paper No. 581**, Institute for Social and Economic Change, Bangalore, June 2024, ISBN 978-93-93879-79-0.
- **URL:** https://www.isec.ac.in/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/WP-581-Dinu-Raj-and-Marchang-Reimeingam-Final-1.pdf
- **Population / geography / sample / method:** **Bangalore (Karnataka, South India)**; **50 employers**, **snowball (non-probability) sampling**, data collected August–September 2023; descriptive percentages.
- **What it actually measures:** Employer **perceptions** of MBA graduates' skills (and specifically because the authors sampled Tier-II/private institutions).
- **What it does NOT prove:** It is a small, non-random, single-city sample of employers about **MBA** graduates; results cannot be generalised to India, to South India, or to school learners. It is employer opinion, not a skills measurement.
- **Publication:** **Background**, or at most a carefully-attributed "a small 2024 Bangalore employer study" mention. It is the best-available *South India* signal, and its limits must travel with it.

## R8 — Graduate employability (India Skills Report 2025)

- **Exact claims:** Overall Indian graduate employability as measured by the Global Employability Test is reported as **54.81% for 2025**, up from **51.25% (2024)** and **50.3% (2023)**, versus **33% a decade ago**; and the report ranks **Karnataka 4th** and **Kerala 5th (71%)** among states for employability.
- **Source:** *India Skills Report 2025* (12th edition), Wheebox (an ETS company) with CII, AICTE, AIU and Taggd.
- **URLs:** https://wheebox.com/assets/pdf/ISR_Report_2025.pdf · https://indiaskillsreport.com/news-pr.htm
- **Population / geography / sample / method:** **>6.5 lakh candidates** who took Wheebox's *own* Global Employability Test across 7,000+ institutions, plus a survey of **1,000+ corporations** across 15 industries.
- **What it actually measures:** An **industry-defined, vendor-scored composite** of "employability" — not a government statistic and **not an English-specific measure**.
- **What it does NOT prove:** It does not measure English communication, and it is produced by a commercial assessment vendor with an interest in the metric. It must not be published as a national fact.
- **Publication:** **Background / Do-not-publish as a site statistic.** Usable only if the founder chooses, with explicit attribution and framing — and never as an English claim.

## R9 — Policy context: NEP 2020 (Government of India)

- **Exact claims/provisions relevant to LanXgrow:** the medium of instruction until at least Grade 5, "but preferably till Grade 8 and beyond", is to be the **home language / mother tongue / regional language**; "**a language does not need to be the medium of instruction for it to be taught and learned well**"; the three-language formula continues with "greater flexibility" and "**no language will be imposed on any State**"; and **"oral and written communication"** is explicitly listed among the skills all students should learn (§4.23). The policy also directs a move away from rote learning toward "real understanding" (§4.4).
- **Source:** *National Education Policy 2020*, Ministry of Education, Government of India.
- **URLs:** https://www.education.gov.in/sites/upload_files/mhrd/files/NEP_Final_English_0.pdf · https://www.education.gov.in/nep/nep2020_final_eng.pdf
- **Population / geography / sample / method:** National policy document (not a survey).
- **What it actually measures:** Stated national policy intent.
- **What it does NOT prove:** NEP 2020 is **not** an "English-first" policy; it foregrounds mother-tongue instruction and multilingualism. It must not be cited as endorsing English-medium schooling or as evidence of outcomes.
- **Publication:** **P-About / Background** — valuable for aligning LanXgrow's language with national policy (communicative skills, multilingual respect), and for the important caution that LanXgrow should not position itself against mother-tongue learning.

## R10 — Regional (South India) employability skills studies

- **Claims:** A Kerala study of **100 employers** and **377 MBA students** concludes employability skills are "**average**", with communication among the lowest-rated (mean ≈3.03/5). A separate Tamil Nadu study reports soft-skill deficits as the largest contributor to the employability gap.
- **Sources:** S.K. Manju Das & R. Velmurugan, *Skill for Employability among MBA Students … Kerala* (institution-hosted PDF): https://www.alberts.edu.in/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/62.-Manju.pdf · Tamil Nadu skill-gap study, *Journal of Informatics Education and Research* (2025), abstract only.
- **Method:** Small, convenience-sampled, self-reported; some are lower-tier or non-peer-reviewed venues; the Tamil Nadu paper was read in abstract only.
- **What it does NOT prove:** These are not representative regional statistics and some are not peer-reviewed. They indicate *repeated directional signals*, not established fact.
- **Publication:** **Background only.** Do not publish figures from these.
